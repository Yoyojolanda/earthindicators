Slow signals

Some of the most important changes can only be measured slowly: emissions are added up from national energy statistics, seawater is sampled from research ships, instruments on the sea floor are read out when a ship recovers them, and species are reassessed one group at a time. The indicators on this page are updated once a year or less often, so their latest value is often a year or two old. That is how these measurements work, not a sign that the page has stopped updating. Each section says how far the data reach, how often new data are published, and when new data last arrived here. The sources are checked every week.

Fossil CO₂ emissions

    Carbon dioxide released each year by burning coal, oil and gas, by cement production (limestone gives off CO₂ when it is heated) and by flaring gas at oil wells, in billions of tonnes of CO₂. The world total includes international shipping and aviation. Not included: CO₂ from deforestation and other land-use change, which adds roughly another tenth. These are the emissions that drive the rising CO₂ in the air (see the CO₂ page). Data: Global Carbon Project, fossil CO₂ emissions dataset (Andrew and Peters), the data behind the yearly Global Carbon Budget, published on globalcarbonbudget.org and Zenodo (the version used is named in the line above the chart).

    Ocean acidification

      The ocean takes up about a quarter of the CO₂ we emit. Dissolved CO₂ forms carbonic acid, so seawater slowly becomes less alkaline: its pH falls. Seawater is still alkaline (above pH 7); "acidification" describes the direction of change. pH is a logarithmic scale, so a small step is a big change: a drop of 0.1 means about 26% more hydrogen ions, the particles that make water acidic. Shown: surface water (0–30 m) at Station ALOHA, 100 km north of Oʻahu, sampled by research ship about once a month since 1988. Dots: pH calculated from the measured carbon and alkalinity of each sample (the full record); measured directly where available. Line: the straight-line trend. Data: Hawaii Ocean Time-series, University of Hawaiʻi (Dore et al. 2009, updated), hahana.soest.hawaii.edu/hot.

      Atlantic overturning circulation (AMOC)

        The AMOC carries warm surface water north in the Atlantic and cold deep water back south; it keeps northwestern Europe milder than other places at the same latitude and affects rainfall patterns on both sides of the Atlantic. The RAPID array measures it at 26.5°N with moorings across the whole Atlantic, from Florida to Africa, combined with a cable measuring the Florida Current and satellite winds. Strength is in sverdrups (1 Sv = 1 million cubic metres of water per second). Shown: monthly means and the 12-month average. Data: RAPID-MOCHA-WBTS AMOC monitoring project (NOC, University of Miami, NOAA), rapid.ac.uk.

        Extinction risk: the Red List Index

          The Red List Index follows how the extinction risk of whole groups of species changes over time, based on the IUCN Red List. It runs from 1 (every species in the groups is of Least Concern) to 0 (all extinct). It only counts genuine changes in status, not changes caused by better knowledge, so a falling line means species really are moving closer to extinction. It is based on the groups that have been fully assessed more than once, such as mammals, birds, amphibians, corals and cycads. Data: IUCN and BirdLife International, via the UN Sustainable Development Goals database (indicator 15.5.1, unstats.un.org/sdgs/dataportal).
