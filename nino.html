    The Pacific map: this year and past strong El Niños

    The charts above follow one box in the middle of the Pacific. A strong El Niño also warms the coast of South America: winds that normally push warm water west weaken, waves of warm water (Kelvin waves) run east along the equator and then north and south along the coast, and the cold upwelling along Peru and Chile (the Humboldt Current) weakens. The maps show the latest month (or the latest day) next to the same month in the three strongest El Niños since satellites started measuring, on the same 1991–2020 baseline and color scale.

The picture blends neighboring grid points; the value shown when you point at the map is the grid point's own. Gray is land (Natural Earth outline), near-white is sea ice (at least 15% ice cover, from NOAA's ice data in the same product). The map is a Robinson projection centered on 160°W; it opens on the Pacific, and you can drag across a map (pinch on a phone) to zoom in, or use the buttons above to show the whole globe; both maps always show the same area. The dashed box is Niño 3.4 (the box in the charts above); the regional numbers are area-weighted averages of the ocean points inside each box: Niño 1+2 is NOAA's coastal box (0–10°S, 90–80°W); "Peru–Chile coast" is 10–20°S east of 80°W, the warm "second arm" further south. They are averaged from the map's 0.2 °C steps, so they can differ from NOAA's own indexes by a few hundredths of a degree. The first map shows the last complete month (it appears a few days into the next month) or, with "Latest day", the latest single day compared with the 1991–2020 average for that day; the second map is always a monthly mean, for the month the first map is in. A single day is noisier than a month, and recent days are preliminary. The colors stay pale up to ±2 °C and change fastest between 2 and 3 °C, where the coastal warming off South America sits, so it stands out from the broadly warm Pacific without the equatorial core running off the scale.

      El Niño and the hurricane seasons

      El Niño warms the eastern and central Pacific and changes the winds high over the Atlantic. That usually makes the Pacific hurricane season busier and the Atlantic season quieter; La Niña tends to do the opposite. The charts add up each season's accumulated cyclone energy (ACE), which counts every storm by how strong it was and how long it lasted, so one long-lived major hurricane weighs far more than several short tropical storms.

       

       

      Most of the swing from one season to the next comes from El Niño, La Niña and other natural patterns, and the yearly totals show no clear long-term trend. Warming shows up in how storms behave instead: a larger share become the most intense, they strengthen faster and they bring more rain (see Hurricanes aren't getting worse). ACE = sum of the squared maximum wind (knots) every 6 hours while a storm is at least a tropical storm (34 kt), divided by 10,000. Northeast Pacific includes the central Pacific up to the date line. Data: NOAA NCEI IBTrACS v04r01, with NHC's operational data for the current season, which is replaced by best-track data after review.

      El Niño over 900 years

      Thermometers measure the Niño 3.4 region since the 1870s. Before that, tree rings fill in: El Niño changes rain and drought around the Pacific (the southwestern US, Mexico, Indonesia), and old trees record those wet and dry years in the width of their rings. Cook and Cane (2024) used this to rebuild the Pacific's winter sea-surface temperature back to the year 1100. Below, every winter since then, with the measured winters on top.

      Each value is the December–February average over the Niño 3.4 box (5°S–5°N, 170°W–120°W), compared with the measured 1871–2000 average. Tree rings: Cook and Cane (2024), the version with more tree-ring records (from 1500) and the longer one before that; each reconstructed winter has an error of about ±0.5 °C, and like most reconstructions it tends to understate the largest events. Measured: the gridded sea-surface temperatures that come with the reconstruction (1871–1981), then NOAA OISST (from 1982), shifted to the same reference using the winters both cover (1983–2000). A winter average is lower than the peak month of the same event. Data: Cook, E.R. and Cane, M.A. (2024), Tree Rings Reveal ENSO in the Last Millennium, Geophysical Research Letters, doi:10.1029/2024GL109759, via NOAA NCEI Paleoclimatology.
