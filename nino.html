    El Niño and the hurricane seasons

    El Niño warms the eastern and central Pacific and changes the winds high over the Atlantic. That usually makes the Pacific hurricane season busier and the Atlantic season quieter; La Niña tends to do the opposite. The charts add up each season's accumulated cyclone energy (ACE), which counts every storm by how strong it was and how long it lasted, so one long-lived major hurricane weighs far more than several short tropical storms.

    Most of the swing from one season to the next comes from El Niño, La Niña and other natural patterns, and the yearly totals show no clear long-term trend. Warming shows up in how storms behave instead: a larger share become the most intense, they strengthen faster and they bring more rain (see Hurricanes aren't getting worse). ACE = sum of the squared maximum wind (knots) every 6 hours while a storm is at least a tropical storm (34 kt), divided by 10,000. Northeast Pacific includes the central Pacific up to the date line. Data: NOAA NCEI IBTrACS v04r01, with NHC's operational data for the current season, which is replaced by best-track data after review.
