Interpretation

    Further reading: Zack Labe's climate graphics, with detailed, regularly updated figures on Arctic sea ice (extent, thickness and volume), Arctic temperatures and more.

    Extent = total area where sea-ice concentration is at least 15%. Each value is a 5-day trailing mean of daily extent; the newest days are near-real-time and may be revised. Reference period 1981–2010, as used by NSIDC. Data: Sea Ice Index, Version 4 (NSIDC / NOAA), nsidc.org/data/g02135.

    Volume = thickness × ice concentration × area, summed over the polar ocean. Model: Mercator Ocean's GLORYS12 global ocean reanalysis (monthly, 1993 to about two months ago), which combines an ocean and sea-ice model with observations, much as ERA5 does for the atmosphere. Satellite: ice thickness from ESA's CryoSat-2 radar altimeter merged with SMOS (thin ice), sampled weekly. Radar cannot see through summer melt ponds, so the satellite record covers only the cold season (Arctic October–April, Antarctic roughly April–October). The shaded band adds up the uncertainty of every grid cell, a cautious upper bound. Antarctic thickness is harder to measure: heavy snow pushes the ice down and floods it. Data: Copernicus Marine Service, data.marine.copernicus.eu.

    Snow on northern land

    Snow covers up to half of the land in the Northern Hemisphere in winter. Like sea ice, fresh snow reflects most sunlight back to space; when it melts earlier, the darker ground underneath absorbs that energy and warms further. Snow cover has been mapped every week since 1966, from satellite images.

     

    Season:

      Snow cover = the land area in the Northern Hemisphere where analysts, working from satellite images, mapped snow on the ground that week, on a grid of cells about 100–200 km across (a cell counts as covered when at least half of it is snow-covered). Greenland, which is almost all ice sheet, is included and changes little; the swings come from North America and Eurasia. A few weeks in 1968–1971 are missing. The weekly chart compares each week with the same week in every year since 1967; the seasonal chart compares each year's average for those months with the 1991–2020 average. Data: NOAA Climate Data Record of Northern Hemisphere Snow Cover Extent, version 1 (Robinson et al., Rutgers University Global Snow Lab; doi:10.7289/V5N014G9), via NOAA NCEI; the same numbers as the Rutgers Global Snow Lab's tables.
