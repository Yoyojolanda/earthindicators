Interpretation

    Most fire CO₂ comes back out of the air again as plants regrow: grass in a savanna within a year or two, a forest over decades. So fires add to the CO₂ in the air for good mainly where forests and peatlands burn and do not grow back as they were (deforestation fires, the drained peat of Indonesia, and more and more the northern forests and permafrost soils, which store carbon built up over centuries). Fire smoke also harms health far from the fires, and soot darkens snow and ice.

    How it is measured: satellites (NASA's MODIS and VIIRS instruments) see the heat given off by active fires several times a day; from the fire's power, models estimate how much vegetation burns and how much CO₂ and other gases it releases. The Global Wildfire Information System (GWIS), run by the European Commission's Joint Research Centre within the Copernicus programme, adds these estimates up per country and region and per week, from 2003. Weeks count from 1 January (week 1 = 1–7 January; 31 December is not counted). The newest weeks can still be revised. Estimates from satellites have large uncertainties (tens of percent for a single region and year), but the differences between years and regions are robust. Regions: the continents and macro-regions as GWIS defines them (Europe includes all of Russia; North America includes Canada, the United States and Mexico); "Brazil's Legal Amazon" is the official administrative area of the Amazon in Brazil; "the Arctic" is the area of the Arctic Monitoring and Assessment Programme, which includes much of the boreal forest. Data: Global Wildfire Information System, GWIS statistics portal (Copernicus Emergency Management Service, European Commission Joint Research Centre).
