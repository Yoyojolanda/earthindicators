Interpretation

    Total column water vapor: all the water vapor above each square meter of Earth's surface, from the ground to the top of the atmosphere, averaged over the whole globe (weighted by area). 1 kg/m² equals a 1 mm layer of liquid water, which is why it is also called precipitable water. "Normal" is the 1991–2020 average for the same calendar month. ERA5 is a reanalysis: millions of observations (weather balloons, satellites, ships, stations) combined into one picture of the atmosphere for every hour since 1940, with a weather model filling the gaps between them. Before satellites (1979) it has far fewer humidity observations to work with, so the early decades are less certain. Monthly values appear about 5 days after the month ends; the newest months can still be revised slightly. Data: Copernicus Climate Change Service, ERA5 monthly averaged data on single levels (doi:10.24381/cds.f17050d7).

    Soil moisture: drought and flash droughts

    Warmer air also pulls more water out of the ground. Soil moisture is the water plants can reach: when the top meter of soil dries out, crops wilt, pastures fail and wildfires spread more easily. A flash drought is one that sets in within weeks instead of months, usually when a heatwave meets a dry spell; it gives farmers little time to react. The 2012 flash drought in the U.S. Midwest left the U.S. corn harvest about a quarter smaller than expected that spring.

     

      Calculated by a model, not measured. Soil moisture cannot be measured everywhere. ERA5 calculates it with its land-surface model, from the reanalysis weather (rain, sunshine, heat, wind and humidity), which is corrected by observations from weather stations, balloons and satellites. Like the other ERA5 numbers on this site, it is an estimate; before satellites (1979) there were fewer observations to correct it, so the early years are less certain (shaded in the long-term chart). Satellites measure only the top few centimeters of soil, so for the root zone a model like this is the usual source.

      Shown: root-zone soil moisture, the water in the top meter of soil (ERA5's soil layers 0–7, 7–28 and 28–100 cm, averaged by thickness), one value a day at 12:00 UTC, averaged into five-day periods ("pentads", 73 a year), on a 1° grid over the land between 60°S and 75°N. For every place and pentad, "normal" and the 20th and 40th percentiles are taken from 1991–2020. In drought: soil moisture below its 20th percentile for the time of year (in 1991–2020 that is true 20% of the time by definition, so 20% of the land is "normal"). Flash drought: soil moisture falls from above its 40th percentile to below its 20th within 20 days or less (Ford and Labosier 2017) and stays below it for at least 15 days. How many flash droughts are counted depends a lot on the exact definition, and studies use several, so compare the years with each other rather than reading the level as exact. Only places and times where the soil is not frozen count, and places and times where soil moisture hardly changes, or where its driest values all sit at the model's lower limit (ice sheets, deserts, dry seasons in dry places), are left out; the shares are of the land that counts, by area. Data: Copernicus ERA5 (ECMWF), read from Google's public ARCO-ERA5 copy.
