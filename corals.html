Coral reefs

Coral reefs cover less than 1% of the ocean floor but are home to about a quarter of all marine species, and they protect coastlines and feed hundreds of millions of people. They are among the ecosystems most threatened by warming: when the sea stays too hot for too long, corals bleach, and many die. This page follows the heat, the bleaching it causes, and what is left afterwards.

Heat stress, measured from space

When the sea stays too warm for too long, corals expel the algae that live in them and feed them, and turn white: they bleach. They can recover if the heat passes quickly; long or repeated heat kills them. NOAA's Coral Reef Watch tracks the heat building up over 12 weeks for every reef region in the world, and sets alert levels: from Alert Level 1, significant bleaching is likely; at Alert Level 2, severe bleaching and widespread deaths of corals.

 

    Each of NOAA Coral Reef Watch's "regional virtual stations" covers one reef region (a group of reefs, such as the Main Hawaiian Islands or the northern Great Barrier Reef), {N} in all, spread over every ocean where corals grow. For each region and day, Coral Reef Watch reports the highest alert level reached anywhere in the region over the past 7 days, from satellite sea-surface temperatures (5 km): Watch, Warning, Alert Level 1 (significant bleaching likely) and Alert Level 2 (severe bleaching and significant mortality likely). Shown here: the share of the regions at Alert Level 1 or 2. Every region counts the same, whatever its size, and a region counts as soon as the alert level is reached anywhere in it, so this is a share of reef regions, not of reef area, and it runs higher than Coral Reef Watch's own figure for reef area (about 84% of the world's reef area reached bleaching-level heat between January 2023 and September 2025). Heat stress is what satellites can see; whether corals actually bleached is confirmed by divers. Data: NOAA Coral Reef Watch, Daily 5km Regional Virtual Stations, version 3.1 (coralreefwatch.noaa.gov).

    How the daily share relates to marine heatwaves: the alert levels build up over 12 weeks and only count heat above each reef's usual warmest month, so they rise and fall with the reef seasons (northern reefs in August–October, southern reefs in February–April). The marine heatwave chart on the sea-surface temperature page compares every place with its own normal for the date instead, so it has no such seasons.

    Bleaching seen by divers

    Satellites see heat; whether corals actually bleached is recorded by people in the water. The largest collection of those records, the Global Coral Bleaching Database, brings together about 35,000 surveys at more than 14,000 sites in 93 countries.

The surveys were not made at the same places every year, or at random: how many were made, where and when changes a lot from year to year, and some were made because bleaching was reported.       The four global bleaching events

      A bleaching event is called global when reefs bleach badly in all three ocean basins (Atlantic, Pacific and Indian) within a year. There have been four. The heat-stress numbers come from satellites; the bleaching itself was confirmed by scientists and divers on the reefs.

      The Great Barrier Reef: coral cover

      Heat stress and bleaching say what happened to the corals; coral cover says what is left. The Australian Institute of Marine Science (AIMS) has surveyed the Great Barrier Reef since 1986 and reports each August how much of the reef is covered by living hard coral, in three regions.

      Coral cover can recover within a few years after bleaching, as fast-growing corals regrow, but those are also the corals that die first in the next heatwave, so cover swings more and more widely between events. AIMS publishes the full series only as a report, not as data this page can read, so these numbers are written in by hand from AIMS's yearly report (Annual Summary Report of Coral Reef Condition 2025/2026, August 2026) and updated each August. 2024 values: AIMS's report of August 2025. The 2024 mass bleaching, with cyclones and crown-of-thorns starfish, caused the largest one-year drops on record from 2024 to 2025.
