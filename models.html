Climate models

"The models are always wrong" is one of the most common claims about climate change. It can be checked: scientists have published warming projections since 1970, and the years they were predicting have now happened. The first half of this page compares those predictions, and today's climate models, with what was measured. The second half shows what the models say comes next. That part cannot be checked yet, so it is marked as projection throughout.

How to read the charts: solid lines and bars are measurements; dashed lines and hatched bars come from models, projections or estimates.

Checked against measurements

Part 1. How well did past predictions do?

Predictions made decades ago, and the models used today, set against the temperatures that were actually measured.

17 projections from 1970 to 2007

    In 2020, Zeke Hausfather, Henri Drake, Tristan Abbott and Gavin Schmidt collected every projection of global warming published between 1970 and 2007 that gave a time series of future temperatures, and compared each one with the warming over the years it was predicting, up to 2017 at the latest (Geophysical Research Letters 47, e2019GL085378; data on GitHub). The projected rates and the verdicts ("matched" means the difference was within the uncertainty of the two trends) are the study's. The measured rates here are calculated by this page from ERA5 air temperature over the same years; the study used several surface temperature records, so its measured rates differ slightly.

(1988), Global climate changes as forecast by Goddard Institute for Space Studies three-dimensional model, Journal of Geophysical Research 93, 9341: temperatures 1958–2019, where the model runs end, as published by RealClimate; forcing 1958–2017 as digitized by Hausfather et al. (2020). The model runs start in 1958 with the measured rise in greenhouse gases; the scenarios differ from 1988 on. To compare like with like, the measured ERA5 temperatures are shifted so that their 1958–1987 average matches scenario B's. Forcing is the extra energy that the added greenhouse gases trap, in watts per square meter, counted from 1958. Hansen's scenario forcings cover greenhouse gases only, so the measured line does too: well-mixed greenhouse gases from the Indicators of Global Climate Change 2025 (Forster et al. 2026, data), which use newer estimates of each gas's effect than were available in 1988.

          Projection, not measurement

          Part 2. What the models say comes next

          None of this has happened yet. These are projections and estimates from the main international assessments, shown as published, each with its range. What actually happens depends above all on how much more CO₂ is emitted.

          Warming by scenario (IPCC)

          Projection

          Period:

            IPCC Sixth Assessment Report, Working Group I, Summary for Policymakers, Table SPM.1 (ipcc.ch): best estimate and very likely range (90% chance the outcome falls inside) of the 20-year average global surface temperature, relative to 1850–1900. The lines to 2100 are the same assessment year by year, from IPCC AR6 Chapter 4 (Lee et al. 2021; files in Chris Smith's AR6 repository), each point a 20-year average plotted at the middle of its 20 years, shifted from 1995–2014 to 1850–1900 with the IPCC's 0.85 °C; the shaded ranges (shown for the low and high scenarios, as in the IPCC's own figure) can differ from the table by up to 0.1 °C. The measured line is the 20-year average of ERA5, plotted the same way. The IPCC combined the CMIP6 models with observed warming and other evidence, so these ranges are narrower and less hot than the raw model spread. Current-policies and pledges estimates: UNEP Emissions Gap Report 2025 (unep.org). "Long-term level now": the straight-line trend through the last 30 complete years of ERA5, read off at its last year (the same method as the air temperature page).
