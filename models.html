Climate models

"The models are always wrong" is one of the most common claims about climate change. It can be checked: scientists have published warming projections since 1970, and the years they were predicting have now happened. The first half of this page compares those predictions, and today's climate models, with what was measured. The second half shows what the models say comes next. That part cannot be checked yet, so it is marked as projection throughout.

How to read the charts: solid lines and bars are measurements; dashed lines and hatched bars come from models, projections or estimates.

Checked against measurements

Part 1. How well did past predictions do?

Predictions made decades ago, and the models used today, set against the temperatures that were actually measured.

17 projections from 1970 to 2007

    In 2020, Zeke Hausfather, Henri Drake, Tristan Abbott and Gavin Schmidt collected every projection of global warming published between 1970 and 2007 that gave a time series of future temperatures, and compared each one with the warming over the years it was predicting, up to 2017 at the latest (Geophysical Research Letters 47, e2019GL085378; data on GitHub). The projected rates and the verdicts ("matched" means the difference was within the uncertainty of the two trends) are the study's. The measured rates here are calculated by this page from ERA5 air temperature over the same years; the study used several surface temperature records, so its measured rates differ slightly.

          Part 2. What the models say comes next

          None of this has happened yet. These are projections and estimates from the main international assessments, shown as published, each with its range. What actually happens depends above all on how much more CO₂ is emitted.

            Without the carbon-removal assumption

            Projection Recalculated here

            Almost every IPCC pathway that keeps warming near 1.5 °C or below 2 °C counts on removing hundreds of billions of metric tons of CO₂ from the air later this century, mostly by burning plants and storing their CO₂ underground (BECCS) or by machines (direct air capture). Today these new methods remove about 1 million metric tons a year. Here the IPCC's own pathways are redrawn with that removal kept at today's level, and starting from the emissions that actually happened since 2020 instead of the cuts the pathways assumed.

            These are the IPCC's pathways for meeting the targets, with cuts far deeper than today's policies; with current policies warming heads for about 2.7 °C (SSP2-4.5, above). Each line is the middle climate response: a pathway counts as "below 2 °C" if two in three plausible responses stay under 2 °C, so its middle line stays well below it (for the 1.5 °C pathways: one in two).

            Pathways:

              Pathways: all pathways assessed by the IPCC in categories C1 (1.5 °C, no or limited overshoot), C2 (1.5 °C after a high overshoot) and C3 (below 2 °C, at least two in three), from the AR6 Scenarios Database v1.1 hosted by IIASA (Byers et al. 2022, doi:10.5281/zenodo.5886911; data.ece.iiasa.ac.at/ar6). IPCC line: the median across the pathways of the IPCC's own warming for each (FaIR v1.6.2 median, global surface air temperature above 1850–1900). Redrawn: each pathway's removal by BECCS, direct air capture and enhanced weathering is capped at 1 million metric tons a year (about today's removal by new methods, State of Carbon Dioxide Removal); removal by forests and soils is kept as the pathway has it. The CO₂ that is no longer removed from 2021 on adds warming at the IPCC's best estimate of 0.45 °C per 1,000 billion metric tons of CO₂ (band: its likely range, 0.27–0.63). Warming follows the total CO₂ emitted with little delay, so this is a close approximation, not a full rerun of the climate model. Measured emissions: each pathway's total CO₂ (fossil and land use, the IPCC's harmonized series) from 2020 to the last measured year replaced by the Indicators of Global Climate Change (fossil plus land-use CO₂, as in the emissions section below). After that each pathway starts late from where emissions really are: its own cuts (its emissions before new-method removal, relative to its 2020 level) are applied to the last measured level, shifted by the years lost. The difference from the pathway's own emissions adds warming the same way (0.45 °C per 1,000 billion metric tons). Methane: each pathway's methane emissions follow the measured change since 2020 (Indicators of Global Climate Change, as a percentage, so differences between inventories do not matter), then the pathway's own methane cuts start late in the same way. Methane does not add up like CO₂, so its extra warming comes from the simple climate model FaIR v1.6.4 (default settings, including the ozone and stratospheric water vapor that methane produces): each pathway is run with its own and with the corrected methane, and the difference is added. Nitrous oxide, other gases and aerosols are left as each pathway has them. Measured temperature: ERA5 yearly means, as above.

              Emissions vs the path to the targets

              Measured Required path

                Measured: total greenhouse gas emissions by gas, in billions of metric tons of CO₂-equivalent (each gas weighted by how much it warms over 100 years), from the Indicators of Global Climate Change 2025 (Lamb et al., in Forster et al. 2026, data). Fluorinated gases are in the data from 1990 (about 1% of the total then). Required paths: IPCC AR6 Synthesis Report, Table SPM.1 (ipcc.ch): the median cut from 2019 levels in the scenarios that limit warming to 1.5 °C and to 2 °C. "At least an even chance" (1.5 °C) and "at least two in three" (2 °C) refer to how strongly the climate reacts, which is not known exactly: for each scenario, at least half (or two-thirds) of the plausible climate responses stay below the limit. "No or limited overshoot" means passing 1.5 °C by at most about 0.1 °C, for up to several decades, before coming back below it by 2100, for 2030, 2035, 2040 and 2050. The straight lines between those years are a simplification; the IPCC gives only those years, each with a range (in the tooltips). Green band and purple path: UNEP Emissions Gap Report 2026, The Point of Return (unep.org), whose cuts are given from 2025 levels, for 2030, 2035 and (purple) 2040; they start here from this chart's 2025 value, and the straight lines between those years are again our simplification. Grey: earlier UNEP paths.
