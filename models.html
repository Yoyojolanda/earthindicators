Climate models

"The models are always wrong" is one of the most common claims about climate change. It can be checked: scientists have published warming projections since 1970, and the years they were predicting have now happened. The first half of this page compares those predictions, and today's climate models, with what was measured. The second half shows what the models say comes next. That part cannot be checked yet, so it is marked as projection throughout.

How to read the charts: solid lines and bars are measurements; dashed lines and hatched bars come from models, projections or estimates.

Checked against measurements

Part 1. How well did past predictions do?

Predictions made decades ago, and the models used today, set against the temperatures that were actually measured.

17 projections from 1970 to 2007

    In 2020, Zeke Hausfather, Henri Drake, Tristan Abbott and Gavin Schmidt collected every projection of global warming published between 1970 and 2007 that gave a time series of future temperatures, and compared each one with the warming over the years it was predicting, up to 2017 at the latest (Geophysical Research Letters 47, e2019GL085378; data on GitHub). The projected rates and the verdicts ("matched" means the difference was within the uncertainty of the two trends) are the study's. The measured rates here are calculated by this page from ERA5 air temperature over the same years; the study used several surface temperature records, so its measured rates differ slightly.

          Part 2. What the models say comes next

          None of this has happened yet. These are projections and estimates from the main international assessments, shown as published, each with its range. What actually happens depends above all on how much more CO₂ is emitted.

            Emissions vs the path to the targets

            Measured Required path

              Measured: total greenhouse gas emissions by gas, in billions of metric tons of CO₂-equivalent (each gas weighted by how much it warms over 100 years), from the Indicators of Global Climate Change 2025 (Lamb et al., in Forster et al. 2026, data). Fluorinated gases are in the data from 1990 (about 1% of the total then). Required paths: IPCC AR6 Synthesis Report, Table SPM.1 (ipcc.ch): the median cut from 2019 levels in the scenarios that limit warming to 1.5 °C and to 2 °C. "At least an even chance" (1.5 °C) and "at least two in three" (2 °C) refer to how strongly the climate reacts, which is not known exactly: for each scenario, at least half (or two-thirds) of the plausible climate responses stay below the limit. "No or limited overshoot" means passing 1.5 °C by at most about 0.1 °C, for up to several decades, before coming back below it by 2100, for 2030, 2035, 2040 and 2050. The straight lines between those years are a simplification; the IPCC gives only those years, each with a range (in the tooltips).

              The cut needed for 2 °C, year by year

                Year by year: the cut from the latest measured year to the IPCC's 2030 level for 2 °C (see the chart above), spread evenly over the years to 2030, starting from EDGAR's first estimate for 2025. Comparisons: greenhouse gas emissions of countries in 2025 from EDGAR (EU Joint Research Centre, 2026 report, excluding land use); CO₂ from burning coal, oil and natural gas in 2024 from the Global Carbon Project (as on the slow signals page); CO₂ from deforestation and other land use from the Indicators of Global Climate Change 2025; methane from oil, gas and coal: at least 120 million tonnes a year (IEA Global Methane Tracker 2025), counted as CO₂-equivalent with the IPCC's factor of 29.8. Options: IPCC AR6 Working Group III, Summary for Policymakers, Figure SPM.7 (ipcc.ch), the estimated potential of each option in 2030 and its net lifetime cost per tonne of CO₂-equivalent avoided. The IPCC publishes these only as a chart, so the values were read from the figure in its PDF (checked against the report's own totals; see the methods page).
